# Register — direct response

Second person imperative. Problem-first headline. Benefit headings.

**Fits:** solo-professional selling a product or a program.
**Exemplar:** Ruri Ohama (#5 in CMS order) — the Kaizen System launch.
**Source:** delivered copy captured from the live site 2026-09-04 — `ruriohama.com`.

---

## A caveat on scope

This is the one register in the corpus written to sell a specific thing at a specific price,
and it is the furthest from the productized tier's default posture. Two of its habits do not
port and must not be imitated:

- **It leads with numbers** — subscriber counts, ratings, satisfaction percentages. Under the
  copy floor a generated field may not contain a digit unless the client typed it into D1,
  C4 or G1. Numbers below are shown because they are load-bearing to the sentence shape;
  none of them may be reproduced.
- **It is long.** The full page runs many times the ~350 generated words a productized site
  carries. What ports is the *shape* of a section, never its length.

---

## Hero — problem first

> **Every Time You Quit, You Trust Yourself Less**
>
> Stop Feeling Guilty About Everything You Didn't Do Today
>
> *Get the system*

The headline names the reader's failure, not the product's feature. The subheadline is an
imperative that promises the failure will stop. The CTA is three words and a verb.

Note the ordering: the reader is told what is wrong with their life before they are told
what is being sold. Every other register in this corpus does the opposite.

Half the headings are questions the reader is already asking. The rest are benefit phrases in
Title Case with a verb at the front.

## The problem sequence

> **Monday** — Full of determination. This time will be different
> **Wednesday** — Life happens, skip just one thing
> **Friday** — Try to catch up, feel overwhelmed
> **Next Monday** — Rock bottom. Desperate for control again

Four labels, four fragments, no punctuation at the end. The register's most transferable
device: a named sequence the reader recognises as their own week.

## Body copy

> You've been here before. That sudden burst of clarity.

> The systems you've been trying to use over and over are simply not made for you.

> They're built for ideal conditions. But that's not reality.

> Many people believe if they try harder or want it enough, they can overcome these
> challenges. But it's not about willpower. It's about having the right system.

Very short sentences. Fragments used deliberately. The turn is always the same: state the
reader's assumption, then negate it in the next sentence.

## About / founder copy

> I built Kaizen because I was the person who quit everything after 3 days.

> Every productivity system made me feel more broken. They demanded perfection I couldn't
> give.

First person singular throughout — the one register where it is the default rather than the
exception. The founder's credibility is built from a failure, not a credential.

## Feature descriptions — benefit before mechanism

> **Actually Start Today** — 5-minute setup, not someday.

> **Fulfillment > Busyness** — Daily highlight and micro-commitments ensure you prioritize
> what matters to YOU, not just urgent tasks.

> **Overwhelmed? Drop to Simple Mode** — Switch anytime, everything stays synced.

> **Sprint Sessions** — Work blocks that flex with how you actually work. In hyperfocus?
> Keep going. Distracted? Take a break without guilt. No rigid schedules — just match your
> natural energy flow.

Each title is either an imperative or an outcome. Descriptions answer "and then what happens
to me", never "and here is how it is built".

## CTA

> *Get the system*

Repeated verbatim at every scroll depth. The register does not vary its CTA label to keep
things interesting; the repetition is the mechanism.
